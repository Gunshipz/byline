# Patterns in other people's text

For check, review, and polishing text that isn't in the person's voice. The brief's six family tests and rules come first; this file adds only the patterns they don't cover. Each pattern is a signal, not proof: flag it when it does rhetorical work, and leave it when it's literal. The idea for this list comes from Wikipedia's guide "Signs of AI writing"; the wording and examples here are our own.

### 1. Unnamed authorities
Looks like: "Research suggests remote teams ship faster." / "Many leaders now believe this."
Do: say who found it and where, or cut the sentence.

### 2. Threes everywhere
Looks like: every paragraph ending on a list of exactly three adjectives or clauses.
Do: keep only the items backed by something concrete; a single list of three in a whole message can stay.

### 3. Ranges that aren't ranges
Looks like: "from small startups to global enterprises" when no scale is being measured.
Do: name who it actually covers.

### 4. Bold sprinkled through sentences
Looks like: several phrases in each paragraph set in bold for emphasis.
Do: drop the bold; keep it only where a reader scans for a label.

### 5. Chat-assistant leftovers
Looks like: "Sure thing! Below is the summary you requested." / "Happy to adjust any part of this if needed."
Do: delete them; the text starts at its first real sentence and ends at its last.

### 6. Disclaimers about what the writer can know
Looks like: "As of my last update..." / "I don't have access to live data, but..."
Do: delete them, and check whether the claim they hedge is sourced at all.

### 7. Flattering the reader
Looks like: "Love this idea!" / "What a thoughtful point."
Do: delete it and answer.

### 8. Stacked hedges
Looks like: "This could potentially perhaps help in some cases."
Do: keep one qualifier, the one that's true, or state the evidence.

### 9. Upbeat endings that say nothing
Looks like: "The future looks bright." / "Exciting times lie ahead for the team."
Do: end on the last fact or the next step.

### 10. Headings that chop a short text into pieces
Looks like: a 150-word note split under four headings of one sentence each.
Do: merge it into paragraphs; use headings only where a reader needs to find a section.

### 11. Claims of fame or coverage
Looks like: "The project has drawn wide attention across the industry."
Do: cite the coverage, or cut the claim.

### 12. A boilerplate section on obstacles and next steps
Looks like: a closing "Looking Ahead" or "Hurdles to Watch" section with no particular risk, person or deadline in it.
Do: cut it, or list each real risk next to whoever owns it.

### 13. A run of definitions
Looks like: sentence after sentence of "X is a Y", each one pinning down a single thing, with nothing linking them.
Do: say why one thing leads to the next, and let the subjects do something.
