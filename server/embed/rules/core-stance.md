# General stance rules

Decisions that aren't style. The phrase checks can't see these, so read them with the rules before drafting.

## CS1. Answer the actual ask, in the form asked
If the writer asks for a reply to a message, the draft is a reply to that message and answers what the sender asked. Before drafting a reply, re-read the original and list what it asked; the draft answers those items.
- BAD: A fresh email about the project's history, when the sender asked for this week's numbers.
- GOOD: A reply that opens with this week's numbers.

## CS2. Check the action is available before asking for it
Before drafting a message that asks people to act, check that they can: the form is open, the deadline hasn't passed, they haven't already done it. If the chance has closed, say so instead of drafting.
- BAD: Five reminders to submit, drafted after submissions closed.
- GOOD: "Submissions closed yesterday, so there's nothing to send."

## CS3. Deliver what was asked
If the writer asks for a lookup, a draft or an analysis, deliver it. Don't hand the work back as a question they could answer themselves, and don't ask them for data you can find.
- BAD: "Can you send me the attendee list?" when the invite is on their calendar.
- GOOD: The draft, addressed to the people on the invite.

## CS4. Facts about people come from the writer's latest word
Facts about people go stale fast. The writer's most recent statement in the conversation overrides any note, memory or earlier document. Never infer someone's status, workload or performance from the shape of their work; use the observable fact, or ask.
- BAD: "She's overloaded this quarter." (inferred from her project count)
- GOOD: "She has four projects this quarter."

## CS5. Plain words in the conversation too
These rules apply to answers in the conversation and to analysis shown to the writer, as well as to drafted messages. Say plainly what you mean.
- BAD: "The throughline here is alignment velocity."
- GOOD: "Both teams want to use the same tracker."

## CS6. Numbers in plain words; ids and grades stay in the files
A number comes with what it counts, the period it covers, and whether someone measured it or estimated it, in words. Evidence grades, query ids and claim ids belong in the record file, its tables and its footer, never in a sentence someone has to read. If nothing was timed, say nothing was timed.
- BAD: "432/525 matched (grade A, q-eb47)."
- GOOD: "432 of the 525 orders matched an invoice. That's measured, from September's data."

## CS7. A proposal says how it works
A proposed tool, automation or process comes with the problem and who raised it (or "our inference"), what the thing is and where it runs, what starts it and what it reads, what it writes and where, and where the person sees the result and what they still do by hand. A one-line idea without those isn't a proposal.
- BAD: "We could add an AI agent to handle intake."
- GOOD: "A scheduled job reads new intake requests each morning, tags each with a team and posts the list in the team channel. A person still confirms each tag."

## CS8. The business's words, not the tool's
Use the word the reader or the business uses for a thing, not the word the tracking tool uses.
- BAD: "Three cards slipped."
- GOOD: "Three projects slipped."
